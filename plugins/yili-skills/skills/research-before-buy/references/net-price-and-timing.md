# Net Price and Timing — what it costs in the end, and when to pay

Used in Phase 5, after the decision prices are read, and whenever a discount
could change which route or which date wins. (When a sale is what *started* the
question, see `purchase-types-and-scenarios.md` first.)

## Net price (到手价)

A **route** is one complete way to buy: the channel (which seller, in which
place), the discounts applied, and how the person pays. The **net price** of a
route is what the person pays in the end: the decision price, less what comes off
at checkout (a code, a member price, a subsidy, a trade-in), less the money back
that is likely to arrive (cashback, a card's points or offer, a brand's rebate),
plus what the route itself costs. Compare routes on net price, and next to it on
what each route gives or takes away: recourse, warranty, returns, delivery time.
The same item is often priced differently in another colour or another bundle, so
the variant is part of the route too.

## Who pays for a discount, and why

Every discount is paid by someone who expects something back. Ask who pays and
why, because the answer tells you whether the discount is real, what its
conditions are, and whether it adds to another discount.

- **The seller** pays to clear stock, to fill a sale event, or to win a customer
  it expects to keep. The seller also sets the "was" price that makes its
  discount look large.
- **The brand** pays to move units without cutting its list price, because a
  list-price cut reaches every seller and every later buyer. A rebate that must
  be claimed after the purchase is the brand's tool, and it works for the brand
  partly because many buyers never claim.
- **The channel that sent the buyer** (a cashback site, a voucher-code site, a
  price-comparison site, a browser extension) is paid a commission by the seller
  and passes part of it on. The seller pays only the channel that it credits with
  the sale, which is usually the last one the buyer came through.
- **The bank or card** pays from its fees and interest to win the person's
  spending: points, cashback on the card, card-linked offers.
- **A government or an employer** pays for a policy aim, such as a trade-in
  subsidy (国补) or a salary-sacrifice scheme. Here eligibility is the whole
  question: who qualifies, where, until when, and whether the fund is still open.

Because different parties pay, their discounts often add up. Because each party
sets its own conditions, one discount can also cancel another: a code that the
cashback site does not list, paying with a gift card, or a later click through
another channel's link can each stop the cashback.

## Money back is a promise, not a price

A checkout discount is certain: the person sees it before they pay. Money back is
a promise with conditions. It can fail to track, be refused, arrive months later,
or need a claim before a deadline. So value money back at what it will likely pay
and when, and show it next to the price with its condition, never folded into one
number, because only the person knows whether they will do the claim. When two
routes are close, do not let money back decide between them unless its terms are
read and the person will claim it. When money back is part of the plan, the route
must be set up for it: the cashback click comes last before checkout, nothing else
touches the sale, and the receipt is kept. A code from a voucher-code site is a
lead, like a search snippet: only the checkout shows whether it works.

## Is the discount real?

The item's own price history settles it, not the crossed-out price that the
seller sets. Read the history of the exact listing and variant, and look for the
ways a seller-set reference misleads: the price went up before the sale (先涨后降);
the sale never ends, so the sale price is the normal price; the item on sale is a
version made for the sale event or the outlet, with a name like the main model's
and a lower specification, so compare model numbers, not names; or a threshold
(满减, free delivery above an amount) or a presale deposit asks the person to buy
more, or earlier, than they meant to.

## Now or later

Timing has two drivers: where the product is in its cycle (see `market-scan.md`),
and where the price is, in its own history and in the sale calendar of the market
where the person will buy, read against today's date. Waiting is a bet with a cost
on each side. It can save the chance of a real drop before the date the person
needs the item, times the size of the drop. It costs the time without the item,
the risk that stock, a subsidy or a discount runs out, the risk that the price
goes up, and the attention spent watching. A price alert makes waiting cheap. A
price-protection policy (价保), a return window or free cancellation makes buying
now cheap, because a drop inside it can still be recovered. When neither side is
large, buy now.

## What the route costs

The lowest net price can sell off something worth more than the saving. How the
person pays decides part of their recourse (in the UK, a credit card can bring the
card company into a claim; some payment services and marketplace sellers break
that link). Count what the route costs: a membership taken only for this purchase,
an extra item bought to reach a threshold, currency conversion (pay in the seller's
currency, and decline dynamic currency conversion), and interest: an
interest-free instalment plan (0% finance, 免息分期) is free only if every payment
is made on time. Count the hassle too: an
account to open, a claim to make, weeks of waiting for money back. Some routes
break a provider's terms, such as several accounts for new-customer codes or a
status the person does not have. Say so plainly, and do not recommend them.

## Who is buying

The discounts open to a person depend on who they are and what they hold: cards,
memberships, cashback-site accounts, student or employer status, region, whether
they are a new or an existing customer, whether the purchase is for a business.
For subscriptions and services, existing customers often pay more than new ones,
so the new-customer price is something to ask for at renewal. How much hassle a
saving is worth is the person's call. When the best route depends on these and
nothing on record says them, ask *how does this person decide here, and what do
they already hold?* Never sign the person up for anything: a sign-up is their
call, and it costs them an email address and marketing.

## Keep it in proportion

The flip test applies to discounts as it does to everything else. Look for a
route only where it could change which seller or which date wins, or save an
amount the person cares about at a hassle they accept. For a cheap item, the hunt
can cost the person more attention than it saves, so mention a discount only if
it obviously applies. For an expensive one, the price history, the sale calendar,
what stacks and the recourse can each change the call.

## The person buys in their own session

Member prices, codes and cashback tracking work only in the person's own browser
and accounts, so an agent cannot take the route for them. When the route has
steps, give them in the Brief in the order the person must do them: for example,
open the cashback site and click through to the seller, add the code, then pay
with the card that carries the offer or the recourse. Then say what to do after
buying, and by when: check that the cashback has tracked, claim the brand's
rebate before its deadline, watch the price during the price-protection or
return window.

## Worked example: a UK buyer with China access

This is one person's real setup, kept to show where the method looks. Every name
and rule here changes, so verify each one live before relying on it.

In the UK, this person can buy through cashback sites (TopCashback, Quidco),
card-linked offers (Amex Offers) and card rewards, so check each finalist's
seller against all three. In China, 京东价保 refunds a drop within its window.
Trade-in subsidies (国补, 以旧换新) can take a large share off appliances and
phones, but eligibility, caps and whether the fund is open change by province and
by month. 百亿补贴 prices are often the lowest, but third-party shops sell many of
them, so check the recourse. 双11 and 618 now run for weeks, with 满减 and deposit
presales, and price history is on 慢慢买 and 什么值得买. When the two places are
compared (see `sourcing-arbitrage.md`), compare the net price on each side,
because a subsidy or a sale on one side can make the gap or erase it.
