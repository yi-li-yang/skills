# Net Price and Timing

Used in Phase 5, once the decision prices are read, and whenever a discount could
change which route or which date wins. A **route** is one way to buy: the
channel, the discounts, and how the person pays.

1. **What will the person pay in the end?** The decision price is the seller's
   opening number. The net price (到手价) is what leaves the person's money, less
   what will really come back, plus what the route costs: a membership, an extra
   item to reach a threshold, currency conversion, interest if an instalment is
   missed. Compare routes on it, and on what each gives up: recourse (how the
   person pays decides part of it), warranty, returns.

2. **Who pays for this discount, and why?** The seller pays to clear stock or to
   win a customer, and it also sets the "was" price. The brand pays to move units
   without cutting its list price; a rebate claimed later works for it because
   many buyers never claim. A cashback or voucher site passes on part of a
   commission that the seller pays only to the channel it credits with the sale.
   A card pays to win spending. A government or an employer (国补, salary
   sacrifice) pays for a policy aim, so eligibility is the whole question. Who
   pays tells you whether a discount is real, whether it stacks, and how it
   fails: a code that the cashback site does not list, or a later click through
   another link, can cancel the cashback.

3. **Is it certain, or a promise?** What comes off at checkout is certain. Money
   back can fail to track, be refused, arrive late or need a claim. So count it
   at what it will likely pay, show it apart from the price with its condition,
   and do not let it decide between two close routes.

4. **Who has already done this hunt?** Someone usually has. A price tracker
   records the history. The comments under a deal post hold the catch and what
   stacked. A consumer-advice site (MoneySavingExpert, Which?, 什么值得买)
   explains how a discount or a renewal works, but not whether this item at this
   price is right. Most of these are paid when a reader buys, so weigh the
   comments and the record above the headline.

5. **Is the discount real?** The item's own price history decides, not the
   crossed-out price. A discount can be smaller than it looks: the price went up
   before the sale (先涨后降), the sale never ends, the item is a version made for
   the sale with a lower specification, or a threshold or a presale makes the
   person buy more, or earlier, than they meant to.

6. **Now or later?** Timing has two drivers: the product cycle (see
   `market-scan.md`), and where the price sits in its history and in the sale
   calendar, read against today's date. Waiting is a bet with a cost on each
   side: the chance of a real drop before the person needs the item, against the
   time without it, stock or a subsidy running out, a price rise, and the
   attention spent. Price protection (价保), a return window or free cancellation
   makes buying now cheap; a price alert makes waiting cheap.

7. **Who is buying?** The discounts open to a person depend on what they hold:
   cards, memberships, accounts, student or employer status, a region, whether
   they are a new customer. How much hassle a saving is worth is theirs to say.
   When the route depends on this, ask *how does this person decide here, and
   what do they already hold?* A route that breaks a provider's terms, such as a
   second account or a status they do not have, is named as such, not
   recommended.

8. **Is the hunt worth it?** Only where a route could change which seller or
   which date wins, or save an amount the person cares about at a hassle they
   accept. For a cheap item, mention only a discount that obviously applies.
