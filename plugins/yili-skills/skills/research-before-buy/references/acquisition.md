# Getting it: price, route and timing

Decide WHAT first; where, when and how to pay are their own decision.

## Reading a price

An approximate price from any source is enough to find the knee. A decision
price is what a finalist costs at a seller now, read as close to what the person
will see at checkout as possible. Their own Chrome sees their region, login and
member price; a cloud browser is often blocked or in the wrong country; a search
snippet is a lead. So read the finalists' pages in their browser, but do the
rest of the research outside it, and do not move the whole conversation there,
because the research reads many untrusted pages and their browser is signed in.
A blocked page is a result: say so and read the price another way. Check that
the page shows the variant you mean. Note where, when and how each price was
read, and any member price, code or money back the page shows. A price without
that is "unverified". Convert a currency at a live rate and give its date. If
their browser cannot be reached from here, end with a short list of pages for
them to read in the Chrome side panel.

## Net price and timing

A route is one way to buy: the channel, the discounts and the payment. Its net
price is what leaves the person's money, less what will really come
back, plus what the route costs. Compare routes on it, and on what each gives
up: recourse, warranty, returns.

- **Who pays for a discount, and why?** The seller clears stock or wins a
  customer, and also sets the "was" price. The brand moves units without cutting
  its list price, and a rebate claimed later works for it because many never
  claim. A cashback site passes on a commission that it gets only if the seller
  credits it with the sale. A card wants spending. A government or employer pays
  for a policy aim, so eligibility is the whole question. Who pays tells you
  whether a discount is real, whether it stacks, and how it fails.
- **Certain or promised?** What comes off at checkout is certain. Money back is a
  promise: count it at what will likely arrive, show it apart with its
  condition, and do not let it decide a close call.
- **Who has already done this hunt?** A price tracker holds the history, the
  comments under a deal post hold the catch, and a consumer-advice site
  (MoneySavingExpert, Which?, SMZDM) explains how a discount or a renewal
  works. Weigh each by who is paid.
- **Is the discount real?** Only against the item's own history. Look out for a
  price raised before the sale, a sale that never ends, and a version
  made for the sale.
- **Now or later?** A bet with a cost on each side. Price protection, a
  price lock or a return window makes buying now safe; a short wait for a known
  sale is cheap; a real need, or stock or a subsidy running out, makes waiting
  dear.
- **Who is buying?** Discounts depend on what the person holds: cards,
  memberships, status, being a new customer. A route that breaks a provider's
  terms is named as such, not recommended.

Hunt only where a route could change the seller, the date, or an amount the
person cares about.

## Another place

Ask whether another place is much cheaper or better, and whether the gap
survives the cost of getting it. Guess from the item where a gap would be, look
once, and go deeper only if it is big. Compare landed cost from each side's net
price: shipping and the tax on a shipped parcel (verify live), handling, a
warranty that does not cross borders, returns, region lock, voltage and plug,
sizes and standards, and time. Goods carried in luggage owe no tax within the
traveller's allowance, so raise tax for a carry-back only when the trip's
purchases come near that allowance. Urgency or any one of these can force the
local channel.

One person's setup, as an example: a UK buyer with China access can buy in the
UK (fast, full warranty, usually dearest), carry the item back from a trip (light,
region-neutral goods that are cheaper or only sold in China; the warranty stays
in China), or add it to a sea-freight batch (cheap per kilo for bulky goods when
a batch is already moving; about two months). Imported Western brands are often
dearer in China; domestic and commodity goods are often much cheaper.

A carry-back works only if the item can wait for the trip, and it must fit the
luggage; spare lithium batteries and power banks must travel in the cabin, and
airlines limit them, so check the rules. Region lock is the main problem for a
connected device bought in China: it may run Chinese firmware without Google
services, or its app may need a Chinese phone number. Sea freight has fixed
costs for each shipment, so one small item rarely justifies it, but an item
added to a batch already moving costs little.
