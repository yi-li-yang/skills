# Framing the decision

Patterns for reading what kind of decision this is. A decision can sit on more
than one; read the one it mostly depends on. Add a new pattern when real use
shows one that none of these frames.

## Kinds or tiers

Choosing between kinds of thing (cast iron, nonstick or stainless pans) is a
different decision from choosing a tier of one kind. Settle the kind first:
prices cannot be compared across kinds, and the honest answer may be more than
one item, each owning a use. When opinions split sharply, the category usually
serves different uses; find which side is this person's instead of settling the
argument.

## Budget is an output

Rather than ask for a budget, read the relationship the person wants with the
thing. Do they want only the outcome, or do they engage with the object itself?
Do they know they will keep using it, or are they still finding out? Still
finding out: a cheap probe. Outcome only, and proven: the durable workhorse,
where the middle tier is right. Engaged, and proven: at or above the knee, and
not the middle, which costs too much to throw away and is not good enough to
love. Record a hard cap only if one exists.

## When the stakes are small

If price does not bind and the risk is low, judge risk and friction instead of
price and value. Ask what the premium buys: only the brand (buy cheap),
durability (buy durable if they hate buying twice), safety (buy up), or a nicer
daily finish (buy nicer). "Both work" usually means only that both work out of
the box, and the difference often shows later, in how long each lasts and how it
fails, as with a cheap charger that fails unsafely. When overpaying does not
hurt, the only thing that can hurt is going cheap and meeting a failure or a
safety risk, so lean toward the reliable option. If the need
itself is fuzzy, a cheap probe teaches more than research.

## Taste and values

Expertise rules out the badly made and frames the good options. It cannot pick a
colour, a cut or an ethical trade-off; hand that back plainly.

## The job behind the named item

The named purchase is a hypothesis. Searching the job ("ways to get lounge
access", not "Priority Pass review") surfaces what the person could not ask for:
access bundled in something they hold, a membership, pay per use, another
approach, second-hand, or nothing. Match the route to how often they will use
it, count the whole value of an alternative, say it once, and back their choice
if it still stands. A cheap one-off does not need this.

## Type and scenario

The type changes what quality, premium, channel and risk mean. For an object,
quality is materials and construction, and the risks are defects, durability and
counterfeits. For a service, you buy a provider's future work, so track record,
scope and recourse matter most, and a vetted referral beats a marketplace. An
experience is one-shot and largely taste, and its price moves with time. A
subscription costs its total over the realistic holding period; check the exit
before the entrance. A financial product is fees and terms over time: inform,
do not advise. A digital good turns on its licence, region and platform.

Some scenarios change the play on any type. A gift serves someone else's use,
known imperfectly, so favour what can be exchanged. Repair, rent or not buying
at all belong on the table. Used goods vary more by condition than by spec, so
inspect, and let the first owner take the steepest drop. A negotiable or dynamic
price is a starting position. In an emergency, buy safe, fast and reversible,
and optimise the permanent buy later. Values can be a constraint as real as
price. And when a sale started the question, ask whether they would buy it at
its usual price: a sale can choose between options, routes and dates, but it
cannot make the need, and most sales come back.
